A furnace that stops heating on a cold night creates an urgent decision: repair the current unit or invest in a replacement. There is no single answer, but a few factors make the choice clearer.

## Age of the Equipment

Most residential furnaces are designed for roughly 15–20 years of service under normal conditions. If your unit is approaching or past that range, major component failures become more common. Repairing an older furnace can be reasonable for minor issues, but repeated or expensive repairs often signal that replacement is the better long-term path.

## Cost of the Repair Relative to Replacement

A useful rule of thumb is the “50 percent guideline”: if a single repair approaches half the cost of a new comparable furnace, replacement deserves serious consideration—especially on older equipment. Ask for a clear repair estimate and a ballpark replacement range so you can compare.

## Efficiency and Comfort

Newer furnaces offer higher AFUE ratings and, in many cases, more consistent temperature control through multi-stage or modulating operation. If your current system struggles to keep rooms comfortable or your energy bills have climbed, a replacement can improve both comfort and operating cost.

## Safety Considerations

Cracked heat exchangers, persistent carbon monoxide concerns, or deteriorating venting are safety issues that usually point toward replacement rather than continued repair. A thorough inspection will identify these conditions.
